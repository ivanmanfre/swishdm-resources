1. Getting cited is the easy part

Choose this text post or the Getting cited carousel. Publish one version.

At Shenzhen SEO someone asked me on camera what one of the biggest mistakes is that brands make with AI visibility. It's also what my BrightonSEO talk was about. "A lot of brands are hyper focused on the amount of times they're being cited, but very few of them are actually checking what AI says when they're showing up." It's worst on versus terms. Your brand against another brand, or your product against theirs. A lot of the time AI is citing you on outdated resources and saying things about you that are no longer true, and that's what ends up costing you deals. In the talk I said being described as the cheap alternative can be worse than being left out, and I still think that. The first thing we do for a brand is research the words and the language AI is actually using to describe it. Then we put out enough content on the brand's own site and on trusted third parties to reinforce the accurate version.

2. The airport charger story

Confirm the facts and permissions before publication.

One year the FBI warned people travelling for Thanksgiving to stop plugging their phones into the charging outlets at airports, because phones can get hacked through them. We work with a phone case brand, so we were like, okay, our client protects phones and the FBI is telling everyone to protect their phones. There was enough of a link for us to have an opinion on it. We wrote it all up as a press release and pushed it out to journalists. The story did really well. From memory it got the client somewhere around 15 or 20 links from real media outlets, referencing them as the expert. That's the basic idea behind our digital PR. We look at what's happening in the world right now and pitch journalists stories that relate our clients to it. I think link building in the old sense is pretty much dead, and marketers and SEOs did that to themselves. The sites that will take a guest post are basically link farms at this point, they're expensive and they're worth very little. The other ways onto real websites are giving them a huge amount of value or having someone well known attached to the company, and for a big brand neither of those scales.

3. Questions to ask before your new site goes live

Confirm the facts and permissions before publication.

Anyone who has moved an ecommerce store with a couple of thousand SKUs onto a new site knows it can be a nightmare. Plenty of things go wrong in migrations, and companies lose a lot of traffic when they do. A lot of the development teams we partner with don't have SEO people in house, and some of them go into launch pretty nervous about it. That's often how we end up on the project, and when the migration goes well, a fair portion of those clients stay on with us afterwards. If you have a launch date coming up, these are the questions I'd ask the team before it's final. Is there a redirect for every old URL, including the blog? Which pages rank today, and has someone checked each one on the new site before launch? Are any URLs changing because the new platform forces its own structure? Moving from WooCommerce to Shopify is a common example. Who is watching rankings, crawl errors and traffic after launch, and for how many weeks? Even moving a blog from blog.yourbrand.com to yourbrand.com/blog counts as a migration, and done right it can help. We saw clicks on five old blog posts go up more than 200% after moving them onto the main store. If you've got a migration planned for next year, I'm happy to look over the plan with you.

4. Put AI traffic next to your organic revenue

Confirm the facts and permissions before publication.

We have clients making more than $1 million a month from organic search who make something like $20,000 a month from ChatGPT traffic. Some of them still come to us wanting to go all in on ChatGPT. And we're like, no, that's crazy. SEO is really boring to talk about, and it's what's making these companies the money. Where I see AI visibility paying off today is at the two ends. A small local business doesn't need many enquiries, so a handful of AI recommendations can make a real difference. A big, established brand can see proper traffic movement once the way AI describes it improves. For a lot of the stores in between, progress looks like "oh yeah, now it's recommending us for this one prompt". Those are vanity metrics, and those stores won't see a big increase in traffic from them. We tell people this. We're willing to help with the AI work, and we also tell them to look at the bigger picture first, otherwise they're throwing money in the sand. Before you decide how much to put into AI search, open your analytics and put organic revenue next to AI referral revenue for the last six months.

5. Ask ChatGPT which one you should choose

Confirm the facts and permissions before publication.

I get 2 or 3 emails a day from other companies telling me SwishDM isn't showing up in AI. Something we found works a bit better is asking ChatGPT which one to choose, a brand or its closest competitor. If you sell running shoes and it tells a buyer to get the other brand's pair, that stings a lot more than missing from a list of best running shoes. You can also see exactly what to work on from the reasons it gives. That's the work we call Perception Engineering at SwishDM. We measure the exact words ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot and Google's AI Overviews use about a brand and look for anything outdated in them. Then we find the things that are already true about the brand and missing from the internet, and put them where those platforms actually read, on the brand's own site and on trusted third party sites. We only put out things that are already true, and we can't promise anyone they'll get recommended. If you'd like us to run that comparison for your store and walk you through what comes back, send me a message.

6. A portable power brand, five markets and $16.3M from organic (added 2026-10-14; unnamed in v2)

Jonathan must explicitly approve the identifiable client figures before publication.

In 2022 we started running SEO for a portable solar power and home backup battery brand, a company with a market cap of over $1B, across its stores in the US, Canada, the UK, Australia and South Africa. In 2023 organic search brought them $16.3M in product sales, against $266k of SEO spend for the year. That works out at about 61x. 2023 was also the year Google pushed out nine confirmed algorithm updates. The September helpful content update hit a lot of sites hard, and their clicks barely moved through it, then picked up in early October once it finished rolling out. Most of the work was everyday SEO. Hreflang set up properly across five sites with very similar content, broken links crawled and fixed routinely, key landing pages optimised alongside the link building, and 350+ regional links over the year. If you run stores in more than one country, I'm happy to look over your setup with you.
